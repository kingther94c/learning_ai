How your text splits

Each box is one token — one row of the embedding matrix, one position in the context window. Colour marks how many merges built it: pale = still a raw character, dark = a multi-character subword.

What merges buy you

Every merge adds one row to the embedding matrix and shortens sequences a little. The curve flattens fast — this is the diminishing return that sets real vocabulary sizes around 30k–100k rather than a million.

At 0 merges this is a character tokenizer: no token is ever unknown, but a 1,000-character passage costs 1,000 positions and the model has to learn spelling before it can learn syntax.

Every merge is recorded in order, and encoding replays them in that order. Merge order is the tokenizer — replay the same merges in a different order and you get a different, wrong split.

Why GPT can't spell: once straw and berry are single tokens, the letters inside them are simply not in the model's input. Counting the r's means recovering information the tokenizer already threw away.

The merges it learned, in order

Rank 0 was the most frequent adjacent pair in the corpus. Notice the shape of the list: common letter pairs first, then suffixes like ing and tion, then whole frequent words — and note that a leading space is part of the token, which is why " the" and "the" are different tokens in every GPT model.

The training corpus

Small on purpose, so training finishes instantly. A tokenizer inherits its corpus's biases: train on English and the merges encode English, which is why the same sentence costs 2–3× more tokens in Thai or Hindi than in English.
